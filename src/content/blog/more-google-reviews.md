Most small businesses have the same problem with reviews. Customers are happy, they say thank you on the way out, and then they never write a word online. Meanwhile, the one customer who had a bad day finds the time to leave a long, one-star review.

It isn't that happy customers don't care. It's that nobody asked them at the right moment, in a way that took ten seconds. Fix that, and reviews start arriving on their own.

## Why reviews matter more than ever

When someone searches for a dentist, plumber or real estate agent near them, the first thing they see is often the map pack: three local businesses with a star rating and a review count next to each name. Before they've visited a single website, they've already formed a view.

Reviews help you in three ways:

- **Trust.** A healthy number of recent, detailed reviews tells people you're real, active and good at what you do.
- **Visibility.** Reviews are one of the signals Google uses for local search, alongside how relevant and close you are.
- **Feedback.** Patterns in reviews show you what customers value and what's quietly annoying them.

Recency matters too. A business with a great rating but no reviews since last year can look like it's gone quiet.

## When to ask (timing is everything)

The best moment to ask is when the customer is feeling the result: just after the job is finished, the appointment is over or the keys are handed over. Leave it a week and the moment has passed.

Some good triggers:

- **Trades:** the evening the job is completed and the invoice is paid.
- **Dental and health:** a couple of hours after the appointment, once they're home.
- **Real estate:** the day after settlement or a successful lease.
- **Wellness and fitness:** after a milestone, such as a tenth class or the end of a program.

## How to ask (make it effortless)

Every extra step loses people. A good review request is:

1. **Personal.** Use their first name and mention what you did for them.
2. **Short.** Two sentences is plenty.
3. **One tap.** Link straight to your Google review form, not your profile page, so they don't have to hunt for the button.
4. **Sent by text.** SMS tends to be opened quickly, and the link works straight from the phone in their hand.

Something like:

> Hi Sarah, thanks for coming in today. If you have a minute, a quick Google review would mean a lot to our small team: [link]

Then send one polite reminder a few days later if they haven't left one. After that, stop.

## Stay on the right side of the rules

There are a few things you should never do, both because Google prohibits them and because the ACCC treats misleading reviews seriously under Australian Consumer Law.

- **Don't offer incentives** such as discounts or prize draws in exchange for reviews.
- **Don't write or buy fake reviews**, or ask staff, friends and family to review you.
- **Don't "gate" reviews** by only sending the Google link to customers who said they were happy. Ask everyone the same way.
- **Don't hide or ignore genuine negative reviews.** Respond to them instead.

Asking every customer, honestly and consistently, is both the compliant approach and the one that works best over time.

## Reply to every review

Replying shows future customers how you treat people. Keep it short and specific.

**For positive reviews**, thank them by name and mention something real: *"Thanks, James, glad the new hot water system is keeping up with the household!"*

**For negative reviews**, stay calm and take it offline: acknowledge the problem, apologise for the experience, and give a direct way to sort it out. Never argue in public. A thoughtful reply to a bad review can earn more trust than a dozen five-star ones.

Aim to reply within a day or two. It also reminds you to read what customers are actually saying.
